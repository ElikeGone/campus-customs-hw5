Reflection: Problem 10

Everything below is based on our own full run, not on how agents behave in general. Evidence comes from the Expected and Actual sections of the three ticket tabs, the Cash tab, output/resolved_tickets.json, and the audit events for runs 0265e7d38585 (#101), dc8ff4b2039d (#102) and 91cf9172734d (#103) in output/audit_trail.json, which has 96 events for the full run including the reset. Run totals: 17 agent steps, 18 delegations (4 refused by our guardrails) and 32 MCP calls.

1. How the agents did on each ticket

Ticket 101: Bulldog tee

What went well: the facts were all correct and all came from MCP.

  • Inventory's check_stock_and_price found 0 size S tees in stock.
  • Accounting's get_invoice_details(501) returned $840, due 2026-08-28, open and overdue: true against the shop date 2026-08-31.
  • get_vendors showed Bulldog Print Co with has_unpaid_invoice: true and a 5-day lead time.

From those the Boss correctly reasoned that the vendor won't ship while invoice 501 is unpaid. It proposed paying the invoice instead of promising Tauhid a date. It also added a caveat our plan didn't have: invoice 501's description ("Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S") doesn't say how many tees the reprint covers, so someone should confirm before relying on it. Customer Service's draft ("Update on your Classic Bulldog Tee order") made no promises and didn't mention the invoice.

What was inefficient: this was our noisiest run, with 7 agent steps, 8 delegations and 14 MCP calls.

  • Accounting ran three times, called by the Boss, by Inventory and by Customer Service. It called get_invoice_details three times and get_vendors twice.
  • Customer Service didn't trust the brief it was given and called Inventory and Accounting itself.
  • Two delegations were refused: Accounting tried to delegate to itself, and tried to call Inventory while Inventory was already higher up the chain (boss → inventory → accounting). The guardrails did their job, but the attempts show the agents don't know who is already working on the ticket.

Questionable: spending $840 for a single $28 tee looks bad out of context. I still think it was right, because the invoice was already owed and overdue, but the agents couldn't actually confirm it gets Tauhid his shirt.

Shop rules: followed. Cash only moved after Elike's approval ($3,400 → $2,560), invoice 501 became paid, and the reply stayed a draft.

Ticket 102: Rent due

What went well:

  • Facilities used get_lease_and_cash(1) to confirm the notice matched the lease: $2,400 due 2026-09-02, two days after desk.date_today.
  • Accounting checked against the current $2,560 (after #101's payment) and said rent would leave $160.
  • The proposal was precise (kind="lease", for_due_date="2026-09-02"), which is what lets the approval tool refuse to pay the same rent twice.
  • Customer Service wasn't involved, which was right because the notice didn't need a reply.

What was inefficient: 6 delegations and 10 MCP calls to confirm one lease row.

  • get_lease_and_cash was called 4 times (Facilities 1, Accounting 2, Boss 1).
  • Accounting ran twice and pulled in Inventory to ask about ticket 103's possible purchase cost. Inventory said it couldn't establish a quantity or total, so the detour produced nothing.
  • Two delegations were refused: Accounting tried to call Facilities and then the Boss while both were already higher up the chain.

Questionable: the Boss's decision even says ticket 103's purchase cost "is unknown", so it noticed a competing claim on cash but had no way to weigh it. Paying rent was still the right priority, because it's a contractual obligation due in 2 days, but the system got there by ticket order rather than by actually comparing the two.

Shop rules: followed. $2,560 → $160 after human approval, never negative, and lease 1's next_due moved to 2026-10-02.

Ticket 103: Bulk hoodie discount

What went well: this was the cleanest run, with 5 agent steps, 4 delegations (none refused) and 8 MCP calls.

  • Inventory found 8 of 20 size M hoodies (Aisle A), so 12 short, and didn't count the other sizes toward size M.
  • Accounting used check_stock_and_price ($22 cost, $58 list price) and get_lease_and_cash ($160) to show the 12-unit reprint (~$264) couldn't be afforded. The Boss didn't propose a purchase order and called out a $104 gap. This is the rule "cash can't go negative" working before the approval step, not only inside it.
  • The discount, 10% ($52.20 per hoodie, $1,044 for 20), keeps a $30.20 margin per hoodie.

Questionable:

  • The 10% isn't from the database. Nothing in the data supports it; the model picked a reasonable number.
  • The discount is conditional on something the shop can't do. It applies only "if all 20 units can be confirmed", which needs the reprint the shop can't afford.
  • "Resolved" is generous. resolved_tickets.json lists #103 as resolved with human_approvals: [], but Yale AI Club has only an unsent draft. That's our resolution rule (no proposals means resolved) being too lenient, not the agents' fault.
  • Mixed approval signals. Inventory and Customer Service set needs_human_approval=true in their audit events, but the Boss's decision didn't.

Shop rules: followed. No cash moved and the reply stayed a draft.

One caveat about all three runs: they took 7–11 seconds and produced decision text identical, word for word, to our Problem 7 and 8 test runs. That almost certainly means Portkey returned cached responses. The flow through the backend, MCP and approvals was real, but I can't claim these show how consistent the agents would be on a fresh run.

2. Expected vs Actual

The ticket tabs already mark each row as ✓ matches, ✓ + extra or ≠ differs. Here's what I think those marks mean:

  • Order and outcome matched on every ticket. The first agent was exactly what we planned: Inventory for #101 and #103, Facilities for #102. All three decisions landed where the plan predicted: pay $840, pay $2,400, and no purchase order at $160.
  • The delegation count never matched. We planned a short chain per ticket, and every ticket got "✓ + extra":
    • #101 added Boss → Accounting, Customer Service → Inventory and Customer Service → Accounting, plus 2 refused.
    • #102 added Boss → Accounting and Accounting → Inventory, plus 2 refused.
    • #103 added Inventory → Accounting.
    In our plan each specialist answered once. In practice they re-checked each other.
  • #102 "Agents involved: ≠ differs." The plan said Inventory wouldn't be involved. It was, only because of Accounting's question about ticket 103.
  • #103 "MCP tools: ≠ differs" is the most interesting mismatch. Our Expected plan for #103 says Bulldog Print Co "has unpaid invoice 501", so no reprint would ship. By the time #103 ran, Elike had approved invoice 501 on #101, so get_vendors showed no blocker and get_invoice_details was never needed. The reprint was stopped by the cash rule instead. Our plan treated the three tickets as independent; the run showed they're linked through shared cash and vendor status.
  • Every run used tools we hadn't planned for. The Boss called get_open_tickets each time; our prompt suggests it for spotting competing claims on cash. In #101 and #102 that's how the Boss noticed rent was coming due.

3. Where one agent with all the tools would have been simpler

Ticket 102 is the clearest case. One agent with get_lease_and_cash gets the lease and the cash balance in a single call, adds get_open_tickets to check for competing claims, and proposes the rent. That's 2 MCP calls instead of 10 and no delegations instead of 6. Splitting Facilities from Accounting added nothing: Facilities read one row that Accounting then read twice more.

Ticket 101 would need about 4 calls (check_stock_and_price, get_invoice_details, get_vendors, get_lease_and_cash) instead of 14. The three Accounting runs and the two refused delegations were all coordination cost. Every delegation is also a whole new model conversation, so tokens grow faster than the tool-call count suggests.

The root cause is in our design: delegate() only passes a task string, so a specialist can't see what its caller already knows and re-fetches it.

Where the specialization earned its place:

  • Least privilege. Customer Service only has check_stock_and_price, and the write tools (apply_approved_payment, apply_approved_purchase_order, set_ticket_status) are on no agent's allowlist. team.py checks that at startup.
  • A protective voice. Both drafts (to Tauhid and to Yale AI Club) kept cash, invoices and margins out, which is easier to enforce with a dedicated prompt.
  • An independent money check. Accounting's comparison of $264 against $160 is what kept #103 from proposing a purchase order that the approval tool would have refused with insufficient_cash.
  • Traceability. The dashboard and audit trail can say who did what, which made writing this reflection possible.

My honest conclusion: for three small, closely linked tickets, five agents was more machinery than the work needed. One operations agent plus a separate drafting agent, with the same MCP tools and the same approval boundary, would probably have reached the same three outcomes in a fraction of the 32 calls.

4. Three problems the current team could solve

I checked each against the live database and the server's tool list. One honest limit applies to all three: nothing in our app can create a ticket. There's no create route and no MCP tool for it. So each request would have to already be a row in tickets. After that, the existing agents and tools can handle it.

  1. Restock the Crest Mug. CC-MUG-CREST has 0 on hand (size OS, $3.50 cost, $14 list price).
    • Inventory: check_stock_and_price confirms the shortage; get_vendors finds Elm City Gifts ("mugs and small goods", 3-day lead time, has_unpaid_invoice: false).
    • Accounting: sizes a ProposedPurchaseOrder against checking with get_lease_and_cash.
    • Human: approves through apply_approved_purchase_order, which re-checks qty × $3.50 against cash.
    • With $160 left, 40 mugs ($140) would go through and 50 ($175) would be refused with insufficient_cash.
  2. A walk-in order for 10 navy hoodies in size L.
    • Inventory: check_stock_and_price("CC-HOOD-NAVY", "L") shows 14 in stock, so it can be filled.
    • Customer Service: drafts a confirmation at the $58 list price.
    • Boss: decides. No money moves and no approval is needed, which is the kind of ticket our team handles cleanly.
  3. October's rent notice. Lease 1 is now next due 2026-10-02.
    • Facilities: checks the notice with get_lease_and_cash(1).
    • Accounting: sees $2,400 due against $160 in checking.
    • Customer Service: drafts a holding reply to Elm City Properties.
    • Partly solvable: the team can verify and escalate the problem, but it can't pay. If someone approved a rent proposal anyway, apply_approved_payment would refuse it with insufficient_cash. Fixing the shortage needs a capability we don't have; see 5.1.

5. Three problems the current system can't solve

  1. Bringing money in. Our only cash-changing tool is apply_approved_payment (and its purchase-order twin), and both only subtract. No tool records a sale or a deposit, and nothing lowers inventory.qty when something sells. That's why #103's $104 gap and October's $2,240 shortfall ($2,400 − $160) can't be fixed inside the system.
    • Needed: a record_sale(sku, size, qty, unit_price, account) MCP tool that lowers stock and raises cash in one transaction, plus record_deposit. Both should be on the backend-only list and audited like approvals. A sales capability for Customer Service or the Boss could then turn the Yale AI Club draft into an actual order.
  2. Receiving goods and tracking orders. An approved purchase order today is just a payments row (kind="purchase_order", ref_id = vendor id). There's no orders table, no expected-arrival date and no way to mark goods received. Invoices have no line items either, so the question the Boss raised on #101 (does invoice 501 cover Tauhid's tee?) can't be answered. When that reprint arrives, nothing can add it to stock or link it back to ticket 101.
    • Needed: a purchase-orders table with line items, plus MCP tools get_open_orders() and receive_delivery(order_id, qty), which raises inventory.qty. Inventory would own receiving.
  3. Talking to the outside world. Every message is a DraftMessage with status="draft", and no tool can send one, read a reply or create a ticket. So nobody could ask Bulldog Print Co what invoice 501 covers, send Tauhid his update, or find out whether Yale AI Club will take 8 now and 12 later. Those tickets show "resolved" while the follow-ups are still undone. The Boss's 10% discount isn't recorded anywhere either, because no tool writes to pricing or to an order.
    • Needed: a send_message(draft_id) tool behind the same kind of human-approval step we built for payments; inbound tools create_ticket and get_replies; a way to record a quoted price; and a pending_customer ticket status so "resolved" means the customer was actually helped.

What I'd keep and what I'd change

  • Human approval: keep it. Agents could only propose. Both payments needed Elike's name plus confirm: true, and the write tool re-checked $840 and $2,400 against the database before touching cash. The ledger reconciles to the cent.
    • Weakness: the approver was mostly rubber-stamping. When Elike approved rent, nothing showed that it would block #103's reprint. I'd add a cash plan showing how all pending payments together affect the balance before anything is approved.
  • Database-grounded decisions: keep them. Every number in the decisions traces back to an MCP result: 0 tees, 8 hoodies, $840, $2,400, 5 days, $22 and $160.
    • Limit: grounding stops where our tools stop. The 10% discount and the reprint's coverage were model judgment, and only the Boss's caveat on #101 made that visible.
  • Change: the complexity. 17 agent steps, 18 delegations (4 refused) and 32 MCP calls for three tickets is a lot. I'd run fewer agents, pass the caller's facts along with each delegation, tighten what "resolved" means, and give the Boss or the approver a view across tickets so the order of approvals is a choice rather than an accident.
